What is a Grundbuch
and why does it matter?

A.N. Immobilien September 2026 8 min read

The Grundbuch is Germany's land registry — the official record of who owns a property and what claims, debts, or rights are attached to it. It is the most important document in any purchase, and the single best place to catch a problem before it becomes yours.

Here is the honest truth: problems found in the Grundbuch before you make an offer are leverage or a reason to walk away. The same problems found after you sign are expensive, stressful, and often impossible to undo. So this document is worth understanding.

What is actually in it?

The Grundbuch has three sections, and each tells you something different.

Section I — who owns it

This records the current owner and how they acquired the property. It is how you confirm that the person selling actually has the right to sell. Usually straightforward — but occasionally you find joint owners who all need to consent, or an inherited property where the estate has not been fully settled.

Section II — the rights and restrictions

This is where the important problems hide. Section II records non-financial claims against the property that transfer to you when you buy. The ones to watch for:

  • Wohnrecht — someone has the legal right to live in the property, regardless of who owns it. Often an elderly relative of the seller. This can make a home nearly unusable for your own purposes
  • Nießbrauch — a usufruct: someone can use the property and collect its income, even though you own it. Common when a parent transferred a home to a child but kept the right to live there
  • Vorkaufsrecht — a right of first refusal. A third party can step in and buy at your agreed price, potentially derailing your purchase
  • Wegerecht — a right of way giving others access across the property
  • Zustimmungsvorbehalt — a requirement that a third party (often the WEG community) must approve certain sales or rentals

This is the key point: entries in Section II do not vanish when the property changes hands. A Wohnrecht or Nießbrauch comes with the home. You are bound by whatever is registered — which is exactly why you check this before, not after.

Section III — the mortgages and debts

This records financial charges, mainly mortgages (Grundschulden). Almost every property owned for a few years will have entries here — that is completely normal. The question is not whether a mortgage exists, but whether it will be cleared when you buy.

In a standard sale, the seller's mortgage is paid off from your purchase money and the entry is deleted as part of the notary process. Your notary handles this. What you want to confirm is that the Kaufvertrag structures this properly — and occasionally you find non-mortgage entries here, like a charge from a court judgement, that need a closer look.

How do you get a copy?

The Grundbuch is a restricted public record — you need a legitimate interest to see it, which as a serious buyer you have. In practice, the seller provides the extract (Grundbuchauszug) as part of the document bundle once you show real interest. Always check the date: an extract from six months ago may not show recent registrations.

What matters most for Indian expat buyers

  • Buying a tenanted flat to live in? German tenant protections are strong. Check the Grundbuch and WEG documents carefully for any restrictions on owner-occupation, and make sure vacant possession is properly handled in the contract before you rely on moving in
  • Recently converted building? Properties freshly split from one building into individual flats need extra scrutiny — the Grundbuch and Teilungserklärung must line up. Errors in the conversion sometimes surface years later
  • Inherited or jointly owned property? If Section I shows multiple owners, every one of them must consent to the sale. This is a common source of delay

How the Grundbuch and the WEG fit together

For apartments, the Grundbuch records your individual ownership, while the Teilungserklärung (part of the WEG documents) defines what you own, what you share, and the rules of the building. The two should be consistent. If the Grundbuch shows a different Miteigentumsanteil (ownership share) from the Teilungserklärung, your share of any Sonderumlage could be disputed.

One specific thing worth checking: look in Section II for a Zustimmungsvorbehalt — a clause requiring WEG approval before certain sales. Some older Berlin buildings have these. It does not stop you buying, but it adds a step your notary must handle correctly, and missing it causes delays.

You found a problem — now what?

An entry in the Grundbuch is rarely an automatic dealbreaker. It is a reason to ask questions and get protections in place before you sign.

A Wohnrecht for a relative who has agreed in writing to move out can be fine, if the contract is watertight. A mortgage in Section III is completely normal if it clears at completion. A right of first refusal can often be waived in writing before the sale goes ahead.

The rule that never changes: know about it before you sign. Once the Kaufvertrag is signed at the notary, unwinding the deal is extremely difficult and potentially very costly.

Your quick checklist before any offer

  • Get a current Grundbuchauszug — all three sections
  • Section I: is the seller the registered owner? Any co-owners who must consent?
  • Section II: any Wohnrecht, Nießbrauch, Vorkaufsrecht, or Zustimmungsvorbehalt?
  • Section III: what is registered, and will it be cleared at completion?
  • Cross-check your ownership share against the Teilungserklärung
  • Confirm the Kaufvertrag explicitly deals with any Section II or III entries

It is dry, technical, and entirely in German. But it is the foundation of your ownership — and ten minutes of proper review, or a professional read, can save you from a problem that follows the property for decades.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Grundbuch in Germany?

The Grundbuch is Germany's official land registry, maintained by the local court (Amtsgericht). It records who owns each property and any rights, debts, or restrictions registered against it. It is divided into three sections: ownership, non-financial rights and restrictions, and financial charges like mortgages.

What should I check in the Grundbuch before buying?

Confirm the seller is the registered owner (Section I), check for rights like Wohnrecht, Nießbrauch, or Vorkaufsrecht that transfer with the property (Section II), and review registered mortgages and whether they will be cleared at completion (Section III). Also cross-check your ownership share against the Teilungserklärung.

Do rights in the Grundbuch transfer to the new owner?

Yes. Entries in Section II, such as a right of residence (Wohnrecht) or usufruct (Nießbrauch), remain attached to the property and transfer to the new owner. This is why reviewing Section II before making an offer is essential — you become bound by whatever is registered.

How do I get a copy of the Grundbuch?

The Grundbuch is a restricted public record requiring a legitimate interest to access. As a serious prospective buyer you have this interest, and in practice the seller provides the Grundbuchauszug as part of the document package. Always check the extract is current, as recent registrations may not appear on older copies.
